The War Was Never Declared — And Started During Active Diplomacy

The US launched strikes on Iran during active nuclear negotiations, without a Congressional declaration of war.

Under Article I, Section 8 of the US Constitution, only Congress has the authority to declare war. Congress never voted. The strikes began on February 28, 2026 — while the US and Iran were actively negotiating over Iran's nuclear program. Just ten days before the strikes, the IAEA director-general said he saw no indication Iran was currently working to develop a nuclear weapon, and noted willingness on both sides to reach an agreement. The stated nuclear justification was further undermined when a US official on a press call the same day admitted there was no imminent nuclear weapons threat — only a conventional missile concern. The last formal declaration of war by the United States was World War II.

The threat from Iran is ultimately their ambition to acquire nuclear weapons, but in the short term, it is the conventional weapon, the conventional missile capability.

— Unnamed US official, February 28 press call

The Minab School Strike — 168 Killed, Over 100 Children

A US airstrike hit an elementary school in Minab, Iran, killing 168 people — more than 100 of them children under 12. AI targeting systems were involved. Accountability has been minimal.

A Tomahawk cruise missile struck Shajareh Tayyebeh elementary school in Minab, southern Iran, on the first day of the war. School staff had begun contacting parents at around 10am to pick up their children — the strike hit at 10:45am. Amnesty International's analysis found the school had been clearly separated from an adjacent IRGC military compound by a wall, and that the strike resulted from reliance on outdated data. The US military used the Maven Smart System — an AI targeting software capable of producing 1,000 target packages per hour — to strike 6,000 targets in the first two weeks. Reports indicate Claude, Anthropic's AI, was also used in planning. The Pentagon has refused to confirm specifics. Forty-three US senators pressed the Pentagon for accountability. The story received minimal coverage on conservative media platforms.

The Senate rejected Democratic attempts to halt the war six times. Congress left for recess when the War Powers deadline arrived. Neither party has meaningfully held anyone accountable.

The War Powers Resolution requires Congress to either declare war or halt military action within 60 days of hostilities beginning. When that deadline arrived, Trump wrote a letter claiming hostilities had "terminated" because of a ceasefire — while simultaneously acknowledging the threat from Iran "remains significant." The Senate rejected Democratic attempts to invoke the War Powers Act six separate times. Even senators like Susan Collins and Todd Young, who said Congress should assert its authority, ultimately didn't force the issue. Congress then left for recess. No formal accountability mechanism has been triggered. Both parties have made statements. Neither has delivered consequences.

The war launched to prevent Iranian nuclear weapons may have made Iranian nuclear weapons more likely. Hardline factions inside Iran are now openly calling for a change in nuclear doctrine.

Iran was not close to having a nuclear weapon before the war. The US intelligence community assessed Iran almost certainly was not producing nuclear weapons. The IAEA director-general confirmed no evidence of current weapons development. Iran was in active negotiations. Now, following two rounds of bombing, hardline factions inside Iran are openly arguing that Iran's nuclear doctrine should change — precisely because Iran was bombed while engaged in good-faith negotiations. The action taken to prevent nuclear weapons may have created the political conditions inside Iran that make nuclear weapons more likely. This pattern — intervention producing the opposite of its stated goal — is a well-documented phenomenon in foreign policy history.
